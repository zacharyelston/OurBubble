# Our Bubble — the beat outline

*One line per beat. Each line is the question a curious reader is asking at that moment; the next
beat is the one she can't help asking next. Prose is drafted to this, not the other way round.
Bracketed notes say what evidence a beat lands on — they are for the drafters, not the reader.*

**The goal (owner, 2026-09-02): reduce complex ideas to simple ones.** The test of *simple*, applied
to every beat: she can hold the whole object in her head at that moment, and check the idea with her
own hands. The difficulty is in the world, never in the vocabulary.

**Two rules for reading this:** mark any beat that arrives before its predecessor has earned it —
that is the only kind of "too fast" there is. And mark any beat whose question you would not
actually ask — that is a chapter narrating us instead of her.

**The ritual.** From chapter 4 on, every chapter has one beat marked ✎ *Before we look*: the reader
writes her guess. Then the look, then the comparison. This is the book's whole method, performed by
the reader every time instead of explained to her once.

---

## 0 · The shadow

1. Why is my shadow short at noon and long in the evening?
2. If I scratch a mark where it ends and come back in an hour — what do I have?  *(two marks: a number from the world, not from me)*
3. So what is a measurement, exactly, and why is it not yet an answer?
4. Who first did the next thing with a shadow — and did you have to be a genius?  *(Eratosthenes: chief librarian, already eminent, one multiplication)*
5. Two posts, same noon, one shadow: what does that rule out?  *(a flat world)*
6. And how much does the ground curve — what did he actually do, step by step?  *(the angle, a fiftieth of a turn; the looked-up distance; fifty times five thousand)*
7. What was the order he did things in — and which step do people skip?  *(notice · smallest shape · say what would prove it wrong · check, keep the no)*
8. Back to her two marks: what is she still missing?  *(a reason to expect one answer rather than another — the hard part, and the book)*

## 1 · Two dots, a line, and the first thing that closes

9. Where could you put a number?  *(a dot)*
10. Where does *change* live — is it at a place?  *(between two places: a line)*
11. Two dots and a line — can anything come back to where it started?  *(no: nothing closes yet)*
12. Add a third dot. What is the smallest thing that closes?  *(a triangle: three dots, three lines)*
13. Put a number on each corner — how many differences do I have?  *(three, one per line)*
14. Walk around the triangle adding the differences — what do I get?  *(2 → 5 → 1 → 2: +3, −4, +1 = 0)*
15. Would any three numbers do that?  *(every time — because I came home)*
16. Is it the triangle, or the coming-home?  *(any closed loop of lines does it; the triangle is where you can count it on your fingers)*
17. Why *exactly* zero and not nearly?  *(plus and minus signs cancel the way integers do)*
18. What did I never use?  *(length — no line has one, and that is *why* it is exact)*
19. What have I assumed so far?  *(nothing: no distance, no direction, no time — a complete little world, not a sketch of a better one)*

## 2 · Which dots, which lines?

20. Triangles tile a floor — what is the triangle's shape in three dimensions?  *(the tetrahedron: four dots, four triangles)*
21. Why not squares or cubes — what can a square do that a triangle cannot?  *(flex: pin its corners and it still leans; the triangle is finished)*
22. Do tetrahedra fill space the way triangles fill a floor?  *(no — Aristotle said yes and was wrong for eighteen centuries; five leave a wedge)*  [picture: the wedge]
23. So every tetrahedral world is a compromise — which one did we choose?  *(a cube's eight corners, sorted odd and even: two tetrahedra threaded through each other, never sharing a line)*  [picture: the two families]
24. What did that choice hand us for free?  *(the two families are mirror images — handedness is in the object before a single number is stored)*
25. Is this the object?  *(yes — everything from here is this one thing, asked a different question)*

## 3 · Make it move

26. What is still missing before anything can happen?  *(a clock)*
27. What is the least a clock needs?  *(ticks, all the same)*
28. What should one tick *do*?  *(one rule: each dot moves toward its neighbours by the differences on its lines)*
29. Is that really the whole law — nothing else, ever?  *(yes; every result in the book is that sentence, run)*
30. Poke one dot — what happens?  *(a ring spreads: the pond)*
31. Who told it to make a wave?  *(nobody; the ring is what the sentence does)*
32. The stopwatch question: how fast is the ring — and is it the same speed every way I look?

## 4 · Is it round?

33. Speed is distance over time — which half do I not have?  *(distance: no line has a length)*
34. Doesn't counting steps give me distance?  *(a step along a cube's edge and a step across its diagonal are both "one step" — until something says otherwise)*
35. So size has to be written in somewhere — how many places?  *(exactly one: the dial)*
36. ✎ *Before we look:* if I give every line the same weight, will the ring be round?
37. What does the ring do on the "obvious" setting?  *(lopsided: 22.4% between directions)*  [picture: the two rings]
38. What if I weight the lines by their real geometry?  *(round to 2.2%)*
39. Is 2.2% the world being slightly uneven, or my grid being coarse — how could I tell?  *(a coarse grid improves with gentler ripples; a wrong geometry does not)*
40. Could a machine decide that without my thumb on the scale?  *(hand it the timings and a written menu of laws; it picks, and says by how much)*  [picture: the fit, the runner-up]
41. Did we ever get this wrong in public?  *(the demo lopsided 3.8× for months — the mesh, not the optics; the switch is still on the page)*  [demo]
42. What did this chapter actually settle?  *(one dial decides whether the world behaves the same every way; and we can tell coarse from broken)*

## 5 · What does pushing on it cost?

43. Now that it behaves evenly — what would it cost to move a *piece* of it, contents and all?  *(the bubble)*
44. ✎ *Before we look:* what should moving the *whole* world cost?  *(nothing — it is just renaming positions)*
45. Did it charge us for that?  *(exactly zero — and why that boring number licenses everything after)*
46. Now shape the push — what came back, and did we put any of it in?  *(three things, all pre-registered)*
47. Where does the cost sit?  *(at the wall, not the middle)*  [picture: the belt]
48. Is it a shell or a ring?  *(a ring around the waist — six times the front and back)*
49. What does "negative" mean here — and what does it *not* mean?  *(below a baseline because of how the shape twists; not a reservoir)*
50. Could ordinary fields supply that sign?  *(no — they never go below the floor; the barrier, and where it sits in our own machinery)*

## 6 · Can you wall a piece off?

51. If fields can't supply the sign — is the other half of the problem softer? Is resistance to being pushed a private property, or a relationship?  *(Mach's question)*
52. ✎ *Before we look:* if I cut a region off from everything else, will what's inside get easier to push?
53. First: does the wall even isolate?  *(half a million times weaker inside)*  [picture: the fading wave]
54. How do I know I didn't just build a machine that damps everything?  *(the outside-to-outside path barely touched: 0.86)*
55. Now the blob inside — does it move differently?  *(exactly as far as before)*
56. Why not — where does a wall live in the equations?  *(at its own location, as a potential; nothing for it to change in the middle)*
57. What is that no worth?  *(under standard physics, cutting the information does not cut the inertia — a bound, not a shrug)*
58. One more way at the same barrier: can aimed fields *empty* a volume?  *(no — energies add; a point yes, a surface yes, a volume never)*  [picture: the null]

## 7 · Can a gap be emptier than empty?

59. Three noes at one barrier — is there anywhere established physics puts a real negative difference?  *(not in the fields: in what is left when you take them away)*
60. What does "empty" contain?  *(every shape a field could vibrate in, each carrying a little; the sum is the baseline)*
61. Put two walls close — what stops fitting?  *(the long shapes)*
62. So which baseline is lower, inside or outside — and why?  *(inside; we removed possibilities)*
63. ✎ *Before we look:* as the walls approach, should the difference grow or shrink?
64. What did the little world do?  *(below the line everywhere, deeper as the gap narrows — the walls pull together)*  [picture: the curve]
65. A sign is easy — what would make this the phenomenon and not a sign?  *(the scaling law and its coefficient with a π in it)*
66. Did the machine find them without being told?  *(−0.9997 vs −1; −0.13099 vs −π/24; nobody typed π)*
67. What quietly changed in this chapter?  *(the machine swept, fitted, chose, reported; a person only checked afterwards — an instrument that can find a law it wasn't told)*

## 8 · Can it tell me something I didn't tell it?

68. There is a way to make a toy look profound — what is it, and why does it never mean anything?  *(formulas hunting a known constant)*
69. What would count instead?  *(a number the method wasn't built to know, no dial to tune, checkable only after committing)*
70. Where does nature hand you such numbers?  *(critical points; universality — details stop mattering, classes share numbers)*
71. ✎ *Before we look:* should I trust an instrument on the unknown before it has recovered a known?
72. Point it at the solved case — what comes back?  *(Onsager's numbers, to six and seven digits — a calibration, not a discovery)*  [picture: markers on the known line]
73. Now take the answer key away — the case nobody has solved?  *(exponents agreeing with real matter to a few percent; no parameter they could have entered through)*
74. What was it still handed, and what happened when that was taken away too?  *(the transition temperature and one class number; the fully blind run found T_c to four parts in ten thousand)*
75. Did it miss anything — and had it said so first?  *(one quantity, too high, in exactly the direction registered in advance)*  [picture: the marker outside the band drawn first]
76. What is left for an instrument to prove?  *(that it can tell us we were wrong)*

## 9 · When the world you built says no

77. What is the simplest kind of prediction to get wrong?  *(a law you are sure of)*
78. Put a denser lump in a resonating cavity — the pitch drops; by how much, as it gets denser?
79. ✎ *Before we look:* the whole-cavity rule is a square root — should a partial lump obey it too?  *(we registered p = −½)*
80. What came back?  *(p = −0.2753 — not a near miss; the law is refused)*  [picture: the two slopes]
81. Why — and why is the explanation written as a *new* prediction rather than an excuse?  *(the lump is a fraction of the volume; if that is the reason, a bigger lump moves p toward −½ — a new commitment)*
82. What does a refusal from the same instrument that found the ripple, the vacuum and the class numbers tell us?  *(that its yeses meant something)*

## 10 · A few thousand years of sharper shadows

83. Eratosthenes sized the Earth — why did the Sun take two thousand more years?  *(not for lack of the idea)*
84. Aristarchus had the right triangle — how could the right method give the wrong answer?  *(87° read for 89.85°: the resolution, not the man)*  [picture: the knife-edge angle]
85. What is the difference between being wrong and being unable to see yet?
86. When nobody could see the stars shift, what were the two readings — and which one held for a thousand years?  *(the null read as absence, not as a bound)*
87. What finally changed — the idea, or the instrument?  *(Rømer, the transit of Venus, Bessel's third of an arcsecond)*
88. Now look back at our two "failures" — what did they turn out to be?  *(the locator's own margin, never carried through; both landed inside their bands once it was; nothing re-run, nothing in the world changed)*
89. Why is that the star that was never fixed — and why is it the rarest thing an instrument can do?  *(find that a published miss was its own bookkeeping, and say so on the record)*

## 11 · Cast your own shadow

90. What did we actually do — in one honest sentence?  *(inspected one object we built, and watched its tools do seven things)*
91. Can I follow one result without running anything?  *(four files, ten minutes: question → data → verdict → summary, in that order)*
92. What travels with the book, and what does the book promise about it?  *(the record, verbatim, checked on every build — and exactly which sentences no program has read)*
93. What is the check that would actually catch us?  *(re-run a test at full size, let it overwrite the data the book quotes, ask git)*
94. ✎ Before you press Return — what do you expect?
95. Where is the next shadow?
