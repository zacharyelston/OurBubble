# Boundaries

> **Scope.** This page is a primer: one scene from the author's own life, told in the first person,
> and the idea it left behind. The book this page belongs to is about a **toy** — a small world
> built inside a computer — and nothing on this page or in that book is a claim about nature.

<!-- beat start-at-the-boundaries.1 -->

Boundaries are good to have. They help define us. Us — and not them, over there.

They are also a good place to start when you are trying to understand this and not that. A boundary
is where a thing stops being itself, so it is the first place the thing can be told apart from
everything around it, and telling one thing from another is most of what understanding is at the
beginning.

I did not learn that from a book. I learned it at a table, from a box of pieces, and the box is the
whole of this page.

Take a jigsaw puzzle. A hard one: a thousand pieces, and every one of them white. Could you do it?

## An all-white puzzle

<!-- beat start-at-the-boundaries.2 -->

If you do puzzles a lot, that looks like a few evenings' work, because you know how puzzles work.
You start at the boundary — the one between puzzle and not-puzzle.

The edge pieces offer the easiest sorting there is at that moment. They divide into corners and
edges, and once they are out of the box you have a hundred-odd pieces to fit instead of a thousand.
You do most of that on the first evening. You end up with long bands that fit together, and you know
neither their order nor which way round they go, but they have one thing in common. Every one of
them has a flat side. Those are the edges. You have found them all, and their final places are only
trial and error away.

So a puzzle falls almost the moment it is started. The border sets the solution in play.

## Tomorrow

<!-- beat start-at-the-boundaries.3 -->

Then the border is complete, and you think: ha. I will finish this.

And you do. Or you get bored, and it sits there on the table awaiting your attention. Tomorrow. Today
you have to have some boundaries of your own. Some puzzles want to be solved; others need to be,
and those are the ones that keep you at the table past midnight.

All puzzles have one thing in common, though. They have a solution — and not just any solution. A
puzzle has exactly one. Afterwards it can seem obvious that it went together that way and no other,
and that is what makes it fun. It can be solved. It only takes the effort of starting to define the
boundary, and the border gives you that start for free.

## Tension and release

<!-- beat start-at-the-boundaries.4 -->

So a puzzle gives you the two things engagement is made of: tension, and release. The tension is a
piece in your hand with nowhere to go yet, and the release is the small click when it finds its
place. Working a puzzle — and the word is working, not solving — you compound those small releases,
one after another, for an evening.

Solving would mean a puzzle is intellectual — that it asks you to calculate something. It does not.
It asks only that you start it, and then keep working, and the pieces do the rest of the thinking
for you: each one you place tells you something
about the ones still in the box.

That is the habit I want you to bring to the next page.

This book is not a puzzle. It describes one — a small world, built one piece at a time — and like
every puzzle it goes together one way. You will watch the pieces force one another into place, and
you will be told plainly where a hand had to choose.
