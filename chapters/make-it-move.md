# Make it move

> **Scope.** A **toy**: one triangle, one tetrahedron, one rule. The tables here are produced by
> running that rule while the book is built, so their numbers are its own output rather than a
> transcription. Nothing here is a claim about nature.

You have a complete little world and nothing can happen in it.

Dots holding numbers, lines holding differences, faces, an inside, a dial: every one says how things
*are*. Not one says what happens next.

What is missing?

## A clock

<!-- beat make-it-move.1 -->

Time. And it is worth being suspicious of that word, because time is what people smuggle into a
small model without noticing.

So do not smuggle it. Ask instead what the least is that would do the job. You do not need a
flowing river of time, or a number that can be subdivided for ever. You need a way of saying *and
then*.

That is all a clock has to provide here: a before and an after, and then another one. Everything
this book calls time is that, counted — nothing in these pages ever measures a duration.

What is the least a clock needs to be?

## Ticks, all the same

<!-- beat make-it-move.2 -->

A tick. One indivisible step from now to next, another after it, and every one the same size as
every other.

Notice what is *not* being claimed. Not that the world is made of ticks, and not that a tick is any
duration. A tick is a counting device: it lets you say this arrangement of numbers came after that
one, and how many steps apart the two are. Nothing more is needed, and nothing more is assumed.

So the clock is ready. Look at what a rule has to work with — the four numbers on the four dots —
and ask whether that is enough to say what happens next.

## Then, and now

<!-- beat make-it-move.3 -->

It is not, and this is the paragraph to slow down on.

One number at a dot says where that dot is. It does not say which way it is going. A ball held at
your shoulder may be about to fall, or to rise, or to stay; the height cannot tell you which. Rising
and falling are not properties of a place, but of a place *and the place before it*.

So the state of this world is not a row of numbers. It is two rows: *then* and *now*. The rule reads
both and writes the next one, and *now* becomes *then* on the way past. That is not a convenience of
ours. The rule that produces every table in this book takes the pair; handed one row, it has
nothing to read.

At rest, the two rows are the same row: that is what *nothing is moving yet* means here. The first
table below starts that way, and says so on its own first line.

So: two rows. What does one tick do with them?

## What one tick does

<!-- beat make-it-move.4 -->

Here is the rule, and it is the only one in the book.

**Each dot's number is pushed toward its neighbours' by the differences on its lines — and carries
forward the motion the two rows already hold.**

Read it twice, because a rule one word away from it is not ours. *Move toward your neighbours*: a
dot looks along its lines, finds its neighbours higher, and goes up a bit. That is ink in water. It
spreads, it flattens, it stops — and it needs one row, because it never asks where anything was.

Ours carries. The push hands a dot no destination; it acts on the motion the pair of rows holds, so
it sets the change in the change rather than the change itself. A dot already rising keeps rising
for a moment after its neighbours have fallen below it, because a motion must be turned around
first. Ink in water needs no memory. A plucked string does.

Is that really the whole law?

## Nothing else, ever

<!-- beat make-it-move.5 -->

Yes. Everything the rest of this book measures is that sentence, run.

The ripple that comes out round or lopsided, the cost of pushing on a region, the walls and what
fits between them, the numbers that agree with real matter, the prediction that came back refused —
all of it is that sentence on a bigger object.

There is no second rule for the hard chapters, and no extra term switched on when a result needs
help. When a later chapter surprises you, the surprise is in what that sentence *does*, not in
anything added.

So run it. Not on the tetrahedron, though — on something smaller.

## Three numbers first

<!-- beat make-it-move.6 -->

The triangle is still there, from before the tetrahedron existed: three dots, three lines, and
nothing in the rule that cares how many dots there are. Put a number on each corner — the third is a
2 here, not the 1 you used before — start it at rest, and turn the handle.

{{napkin:triangle_slosh_table}}

They slosh. And they come home — the pair of rows at tick four is the pair it started from, so from
there the whole thing happens again, and again, for ever.

Two things to check with a finger before reading on. Add each row up against the last column: the
same total, every time.
And nothing settles: this is the plucked string, not the ink. Whole numbers all the way down the
table, too, which is what the third corner bought.

## The tick belongs to the shape

<!-- beat make-it-move.7 -->

One number in that run was neither the rule's nor the triangle's. The tick.

The step from one row to the next has a size, and the size that suits the triangle is two thirds.
The tetrahedron takes a half. Neither is a claim about time; each is a property of the shape being
run. Watch what the triangle does at the tetrahedron's tick.

{{napkin:tick_belongs_to_shape}}

Nothing broke: the total is untouched and the numbers stay small. What is gone is the arithmetic
you could do yourself, and the coming home with it.

That last line is the shape talking again; later you will watch a tick go over a shape's ceiling and
take the world with it. The dial in
[one tetrahedron is a whole world](one-tetrahedron-is-a-whole-world.md) said how much a line counts.
This is that kind of setting, arriving in time before it arrives in length.

## Now the tetrahedron

<!-- beat make-it-move.8 -->

Four different numbers on the four corners, started at rest, at the tick this shape takes: a half.
Then the rule, ten times.

{{napkin:slosh_table}}

The same story with one more dot. They slosh, they do not settle, and they come back — tick 3
holds the same four numbers as tick 0, in the same places. Not close; the same. It does it again at
4, and at 7 and 8, and it will go on for ever.

Count it off before reading on. That the world comes back *at all* is the rule's doing: nothing
leaks away, so nothing runs down. That it comes back onto those exact numbers is the tick.

## The total never moves

<!-- beat make-it-move.9 -->

Now add the four up at each tick and look down that last column. It does not change: not on any
tick, not by a little — the same number, eleven times.

Two things have to be true for that.

**The pushes cancel in pairs.** Every line leans on its two ends by the same difference in opposite
directions: what one end is pushed up by, the other is pushed down by. Add them over the whole world
and they contribute nothing. That is the balancing act from walking round a triangle, moved into
time.

**And the carried motion started at zero.** We began at rest, so on the first tick there was no
motion anywhere and the pushes added none — then the same two sentences on the next tick, and every
tick after.

Nothing here leaks, and that is why the sloshing keeps coming back rather than dying away.

## Turn the dial

<!-- beat make-it-move.10 -->

Take one of the six lines and say it counts double: a difference along it pulls twice as hard as
along the others. Then run the same ten ticks again.

{{napkin:slosh_table_dialed}}

Three things, and the third is the one to keep.

The motion along that line got more violent. One of its two dots now goes *below every number the
world started with*, which a levelling-off process could never do. Overshooting everything you began
with is a wave's signature.

The rhythm got thinner. With every line counting the same the world came home at 3 and again at 4,
then at 7 and 8; this one comes home at 3, then not until 8.

And the total never moved, so neither did the average. The dial changed *how* the world moves, not
how much there is. A later chapter is about a dial set badly, and the tell was never in the totals —
always in the motion.

## Where is the ring?

<!-- beat make-it-move.11 -->

Now stop being gentle. Poke one dot hard — put a big number on it, leave the others alone, and
watch.

There is no ring.

You know what you were expecting, because you have dropped a stone in a pond: a ring spreading
outward, and behind it a place it has already passed. Poke this tetrahedron and the big number
sloshes into the other three at once, all together, and back again.

The reason is embarrassingly simple, and it is a table.

{{napkin:no_room}}

Every dot is joined to every other dot. There is no "outward", because no place is further off than
any other. There is no behind-the-ring, because nothing is behind anything. The object has no *room*
in it.

Which means there is a question you cannot ask here.

## The question this world cannot answer

<!-- beat make-it-move.12 -->

You cannot ask how fast.

Speed is a distance divided by a time. You have the time: ticks, and you can count them. What you
lack is anything to divide by, and the reason is not an unset dial: the six lines were offered
lengths in [one tetrahedron is a whole world](one-tetrahedron-is-a-whole-world.md). Every pair of
dots here is one line apart, so every journey is the same. There is nothing to compare.

Behind it sits a sharper version, and that is the one this book spends a chapter on. Not just *how
fast*, but: **is it the same speed in every direction?** Directions need somewhere to go.

So you need room. The obvious way to get it is a second tetrahedron — but is that the cheapest place
to look?
