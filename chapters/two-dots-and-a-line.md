# Two dots, a line, and the first thing that closes

> **Scope.** Everything in this chapter is a **toy** you can draw on a napkin, and every number in it
> is worked out while the book is being built rather than quoted from anywhere. Nothing here is a
> claim about nature. No physics word appears until the thing it names is already yours.

Start with nothing. Not an empty universe with laws waiting in it — nothing, a blank page, and the
question of what the least is that you would have to draw before anything at all could happen.

Here is the first thing you need, and it is smaller than you would expect.

## Somewhere to put a number

You need somewhere a number can sit. Call it a dot.

That is not a philosophical claim about the nature of space. It is a bookkeeping requirement: if you
want to say *the temperature here is seven*, the word "here" has to point at something. A dot is the
least that can be pointed at. Draw one.

Now put a number on it. Seven, if you like.

And notice immediately that you cannot do anything with it. One number on one dot never changes,
because there is nothing for it to be different *from*. A world with one dot in it has no news in
it, ever.

So where does change live?

## Change lives between

Not at a dot. This is the first thing in the book that is genuinely worth stopping on, because it is
the piece most people carry a wrong picture of.

Put a second dot down and give it a number — three, say. Now something exists that did not exist a
moment ago: the *difference* between them. Seven and three are two facts about two places. Four is a
fact about neither of them. It belongs to the pair.

So draw the pair. A line, joining the two dots, and the difference lives on the line.

That is the whole of the vocabulary so far: dots hold numbers, lines hold differences. Two words, and
you already own both.

Can you do anything with two dots and a line?

## Nothing closes yet

Try to go somewhere and come back.

Start at the first dot, cross the line to the second, and then — there is nowhere to go but back the
way you came. And going back the way you came is not a journey; it undoes itself. Whatever you added
crossing the line, you subtract crossing it again. You learn nothing, because you never really went
anywhere.

That matters more than it sounds. The interesting thing a world can do is let you set out, keep
going, and arrive back where you started *without retracing*. Two dots and a line cannot do it. There
is no round trip available.

So add a third dot. What is the smallest thing that closes?

## The triangle

Three dots. Join each to both of the others: three lines. And the moment you draw the third line,
something appears that was not there before and that you did not ask for.

An inside.

Two dots and a line have no inside. Three dots and three lines have a region enclosed — a face — and
the boundary of that region is a route you can walk all the way round and come home by, never
doubling back. The triangle is the first thing that closes. It is the smallest object in this book,
and almost everything that follows is the consequence of it closing.

Put a number on each corner. How many differences do you now have?

## Three corners, three differences

Three. One per line, and no more, because a difference belongs to a pair and there are exactly three
pairs.

Notice that you did not choose the differences. You chose the three corner numbers; the differences
came out. They are not extra facts about the world, they are what the world already said, read off
along the lines. Nothing in this chapter is ever going to be *added* to the object. Everything is
going to be read off it.

Which means there is now something to check. Walk the boundary of that triangle, adding up each
difference as you cross its line, and come back to where you started.

What do you get?

## Walk it and add

Do it with real numbers rather than in the abstract.

{{napkin:triangle_loop_example}}

Zero. Not a small number, not a number close to zero. Zero.

And once you have seen it once, you can feel why. Each corner number got added when you arrived at it
and subtracted when you left. Every corner is arrived at exactly once and left exactly once, so every
number in the walk appears twice with opposite signs. The books balance because you came home.

But is that special to those three numbers?

## Any three numbers at all

No. Try it with any three. Try it with numbers you pick to be awkward — enormous ones, negative ones,
the same one three times. It comes back zero every time, and it is not luck.

You can see it in one line of reasoning. Going all the way round a closed loop means each corner is
entered once and left once. The sum of the differences along a closed walk is therefore the sum of
each corner number minus itself. There is nothing left over. There is nothing that *could* be left
over.

This is the first fact about the object that is true of the object rather than of any particular
numbers you put on it. It has one more surprise in it, though, and the surprise is the word *exactly*.

Why exactly zero, and not nearly?

## Why it is exact and not approximate

Because nothing was measured, and nothing was rounded.

That sentence is doing a lot of work, so here it is slowly. When a simulation says a quantity is
zero, it usually means the quantity came out very small — smaller than the errors, small enough to
call zero and move on. Ours does not mean that. The walk around the triangle adds a number and then
subtracts the same number. Plus and minus cancel the way they cancel in a shopping list, not the way
they cancel in a measurement.

It is bookkeeping, not physics. The books balance for the same reason a ledger balances when you
record every payment twice.

Which raises a question you may already have asked: what did you never use?

## What you never used

Length.

Go back over everything you have drawn and look for it. The dots are somewhere, but no dot is a
*distance* from any other dot. The lines join things, but no line is long or short. The triangle has
an inside, but the inside has no area. You have been walking around a shape without ever knowing how
big it is, and the walk still came home, and the books still balanced.

That is not a gap in the picture. It is the reason the zero is exact. A quantity that depends on no
measured length cannot be spoiled by measuring a length badly. The exactness and the missing ruler
are the same fact seen twice.

So what have you actually assumed, all told?

## Nothing at all

Take the inventory, because it is short and it is the point of the chapter.

No distances. No directions — nothing here knows what "north" would mean. No time; nothing has
happened yet, and nothing can. No physics: not a force, not a field, not a particle. Just dots
holding numbers, lines holding differences, one face with an inside, and one fact that came for free
when the shape closed.

And this is worth saying plainly, because it is easy to read what you have built as a crude sketch of
something better. It is not. It is a small world, complete in itself, with nothing missing that it
needs. Everything from here is that world, made slightly larger, and always because a question forced
it.

Here is the question that forces the next one. A triangle closes, and it is flat. What is the same
shape, one dimension up?
