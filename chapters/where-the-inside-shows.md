# Where the inside shows

> **Scope.** This is a book about a **toy**: a small world built inside a computer, and what happened
> when we pointed real tests at it. Nothing here is a claim about nature. Where a chapter uses a word
> like *vacuum*, the word names a pattern in the model.

<!-- beat where-the-inside-shows.1 -->

Put an egg on the table and look at it as hard as you like. You are not seeing the egg. You are
seeing the place where the egg stops and the room begins, and anything you ever learn about what is
inside has to come out through there.

That is not a complaint about eyes. It is the position of anyone who has ever measured anything. The
inside of a thing is not handed to you. Its edge is — and things cross it. Light, warmth, a sound, a
smell, a crack running down the shell. Whatever reached you got to you by crossing.

So an edge is not the leftover part of an object, the bit you are stuck with once the interesting
middle has been sealed away. It is the only part that is on speaking terms with you. A boundary is
where a thing becomes observable at all, and that is worth a few pages before anything else here
starts.

## So measure at the edge

<!-- beat where-the-inside-shows.2 -->

Why there, and not somewhere more central? Because the edge is where the inside gets written down.

Suppose something is going on inside some region. If none of it ever crosses the boundary, nothing
standing outside can tell that region from an empty one, because there is no other way out. And if
some of it does get out, it got out *there*. So a patient account of what goes through the edge is
not a second-best substitute for reaching the middle. It is the middle, in the only form that ever
leaves.

That sounds too convenient, and you should hold it lightly until you have done it yourself. But it
is the promise this book keeps as literally as it can: with a pencil, on a napkin. You will draw a
small shape, walk once round its rim adding up the differences you meet on the way, and come home to
nothing at all. Then you will add up the faces that close around an inside, and get nothing again —
every time, whatever numbers you started from. The inside and its edge turn out to be one account,
and you can read it from either side.

## Where the observer is standing

<!-- beat where-the-inside-shows.3 -->

Notice where that leaves you. Whenever you measure, you are at a boundary — the thing's or your
instrument's, and usually both at once.

Nearly everything in this book is somebody reading an edge. A wall put up to cut one piece of a small
world off from the rest, and the question of what still gets past it. The narrow gap between two such
walls, and what stops fitting in there. The faces that close around a middle with nothing in it. And
a quieter kind of edge, which arrives early: the finest difference an instrument can still tell
apart, which is what separates being wrong from not being able to see yet.

What each of those returned is that chapter's to tell, and several of the answers are noes. The point
here is smaller, and it holds for every one of them. Not one of those measurements reaches into the
middle of anything. Each sits at an edge and keeps count of what crosses it.

## An edge is also a seam

<!-- beat where-the-inside-shows.4 -->

There is a second reason to be interested in boundaries, and it has nothing to do with looking.

An edge between two pieces of a world belongs to both of them. Whatever leaves one across it arrives
in the other, so a single crossing is a loss read from the left and a gain read from the right: one
quantity, written down twice with opposite signs. Now do that everywhere at once, over every small
piece of a world together. Each crossing on the inside gets counted twice, once each way, and the
pairs cancel. What is left of the whole sum is only what went across the outer edge — and where the
pieces close up on themselves, with no outer edge at all, what is left is nothing.

Which is to say that the bookkeeping at the boundaries is the statement that nothing went quietly
missing. That is easy to assert and cheap to believe, so you are not asked to. A later chapter does
the sum in front of you, on a shape whose faces you can count on your fingers.

## Where we start

<!-- beat where-the-inside-shows.5 -->

None of this needs equipment. The oldest boundary anyone measured is outdoors, it is free, and it
moves while you watch.

A shadow is an edge. Not a thing in its own right — a place where the light stops, with lit ground on
one side of the line and unlit ground on the other. You cannot go and inspect the Sun, and you do not
have to. Something crosses the gap, meets an obstacle, and leaves a line on the ground. The line is
at the boundary, and the line is what you get.

Somebody is about to notice one, in a yard, and do the smallest possible thing about it.
