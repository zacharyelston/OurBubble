No — the cheapest room in this book is not a second tetrahedron. It has been in your hands,
unopened, since you [counted the first one](one-tetrahedron-is-a-whole-world.md): not beside that
tetrahedron. Inside it.

## Divide it instead of adding to it

<!-- beat 43 -->

Adding is not the only way to get something bigger. You can also cut.

Take your tetrahedron: four dots, six lines. Find the middle of each line. That is six new dots, and
notice that nothing has been added to the object — those points were always there, halfway along
lines you already had. You have only named them.

Now cut through them. Slice off each corner, cutting straight across the three middles nearest to
it, and do that at all four corners.

It is worth a pencil before you read on. Draw the four triangles of the net, mark the six middles,
and join the middles that sit on the same face. Everything in the rest of this chapter is a count,
and a count you have made yourself is a count nobody has to sell you.

Four corners come away. What is left?

## What falls out

<!-- beat 44 -->

Four small tetrahedra, and one shape that is not a tetrahedron at all.

{{napkin:octa_cut}}

The tips are the easy part: each one is your tetrahedron again at half the size, sitting on a corner
you already knew. It is the other piece that is new. Four of its faces are the inward faces of the
cuts, and four are the middle triangles of the original's own faces — eight faces in all, which no
tetrahedron has.

And nothing went missing in the cutting. Four tips at an eighth each account for half of it, and the
shape between them is the other half exactly. It is not a gap or a leftover. It is the biggest
single piece a tetrahedron is made of.

So count it, the way you counted the tetrahedron.

## Six dots, and the first two that are not neighbours

<!-- beat 45 -->

{{napkin:octa_counts}}

The names are not new names. Each of its six dots is the middle of one of the tetrahedron's six
lines, so each keeps that line's name, and you can read the joins straight off the letters: two of
them are joined when their lines share a corner, and not otherwise.

Which is where something changes. AB and CD have no letter in common, and no line between them.
Neither do AC and BD, nor AD and BC. Three pairs of places, in the same small object, that are not
each other's neighbours.

That is the thing the tetrahedron could not give you. On four dots, everywhere was next door to
everywhere; there was no *over there* to reach. Here there is — and reaching it should now take
something.

Same rule, same tick size. Poke one dot.

## It takes two ticks to cross

<!-- beat 46 -->

{{napkin:octa_poke_table}}

Read the second row and then the third. At tick 1 the poke has spread onto the four dots that are
its neighbours, and the dot you poked has gone negative. At tick 2 there is nothing anywhere except
on CD — the one dot that was not its neighbour. The whole of it has crossed, and it took two ticks.

At tick 3 it is home again, and the total across the row has not moved once. Below that it keeps
going — home again at 8 and 11 — and only at 12, at this tick size, does it repeat the pair of rows
it began from.

Notice what did *not* travel. No stuff went anywhere; there is no stuff. What crossed was the poke
itself — a pattern of numbers, handed on by the rule twice. That is worth having a plain name for,
and the plain name is not *time*: what happened here is information getting from one place to
another, and time is only the ticks we counted while it did.

Now put numbers on its lines instead, the way you did on the tetrahedron.

## Coming home, on eight faces

<!-- beat 47 -->

Eight faces this time, and the same walk. What do they add to?

{{napkin:octa_face_sum}}

Zero — and for the same reason as before, which you can check in the table without believing a word
of it. Every one of the twelve lines lies between two faces, both walked as seen from outside, so
each walks that line the opposite way: its arrow enters the total once as plus and once as minus.

You proved this on four faces in [one tetrahedron is a whole world](one-tetrahedron-is-a-whole-world.md);
having it on eight, on a closed surface, for any arrows at all, has cost you nothing. No line has a
length still, so none was used.

Four of those eight faces are still bare, though. Four point straight out at a tip and are covered
by it; the other four lie flat in the faces of the tetrahedron you cut, and are exactly the size of
the faces the tips sit on.

So something tip-sized would fit on each of them. Whether to put it there is the next question.
