# What you will have

> **Scope.** This is a book about a **toy**: a small world built inside a computer, and what happened
> when we pointed real tests at it. Nothing here is a claim about nature. Where a chapter uses a word
> like *vacuum*, the word names a pattern in the model.

This is a page about the book rather than a page of it. Two minutes, and then we begin.

## The thing this is about

<!-- beat 1 -->

There is a subject underneath this book, and it has a name: *discrete exterior calculus*. It is a
way of doing calculus on a shape made of dots, lines and faces, instead of on a smooth space. People
spend years learning it, under names built to keep strangers out. Forms. Exactness. The Hodge star.
The Laplacian. Stability. The vacuum. Universality.

Please do not go and look any of that up yet.

Every one of those names is in this book, and every one of them arrives *last* — after you have
counted the thing it names on a napkin, in whole numbers, and watched it come out the same every
time. A name is a handle. Handles are useful once you are already holding something; on their own
they are just hard to grip.

That is the only paragraph in here that will do that to you. Set the words aside.

## What you get

<!-- beat 2 -->

The ideas themselves, by the last page — not a rumour of them. You will know what the people who
work on this know about it: there is less to know than the vocabulary suggests, and none of it is
kept back.

You will be able to hold a whole world in your head and count it; run the single rule everything
after it is made of; say why a clock's tick belongs to a shape, not to you; read a machine's honest
*no* and tell it from a mistake; walk from a number on a page to the file it came out of; and leave
a note on the exact paragraph that lost you — the one thing here that needs an account.

You will not be asked to believe anything. Every number in the first half you can check with a
pencil; every one in the second half is quoted from a record that travels with the book.

And one thing you will not have, which matters most: a claim about the world. What we built is a
toy — small, exact, made on purpose — and where this book borrows a word from physics, it names a
feature of the toy.

## The road

<!-- beat 3 -->

The first five chapters happen on a napkin. Two dots and a line, then a triangle, then one
tetrahedron — four corners, six edges, four faces — and that small thing turns out to be a whole
world: every kind of number it can hold, and one dial that gives it a geometry. Then we make it
move. You can do all of it by hand, and you should.

Then the napkin runs out, visibly, in front of you: the smallest world with any room in it needs
arithmetic no hand can do. So the same rule — the same one, unchanged — is handed to a machine and
run on a world with no edge at all.

The rest is what came back. A ripple that was lopsided until one setting fixed it. What it costs to
push on a piece of the world. A wall that worked, two hopes that did not, a law that came back
refused, and a published miss that turned out to be our own bookkeeping. The noes are kept on
purpose and given the same room as the yeses, because a machine that can only agree with you is not
an instrument.

## How to read it

<!-- beat 4 -->

This is written for someone curious who is not a physicist. There is no mathematics in it beyond
adding and taking away — which is not a simplification made for your benefit, but what the object
actually needs.

Read it in order. Each chapter earns the next one's question, and the whole thing is short enough
that skipping ahead costs more than it saves.

And do the ✎ moments. From partway in, every chapter that runs an experiment stops just before the
answer and asks you to write your guess down. That pause is the method, performed instead of
described, and it only works if you commit — being wrong on paper is the point of it, not the risk.

We start where measuring started: with a shadow, and a man in Alexandria who did the smallest
possible next thing about one.
