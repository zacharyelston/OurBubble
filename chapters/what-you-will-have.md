# What you will have

> **Scope.** This is a book about a **toy**: a small world built inside a computer, and what happened
> when we pointed real tests at it. Nothing here is a claim about nature. Where a chapter uses a word
> like *vacuum*, the word names a pattern in the model.

This is the one page that talks about the book instead of doing it. A few minutes, and then we
begin.

## The thing this is about

<!-- beat 1 -->

There is a subject underneath this book, and it has a name: *discrete exterior calculus*. It is
calculus on a shape made of dots, lines and faces instead of a smooth one. People spend years
learning it, under names that give a beginner nothing to hold on to. Forms. Exactness. The Hodge
star. The Laplacian. Stability.

Please do not go and look that up yet.

You will meet all five of those things in this book, and almost none of those words. A number
sitting on a dot, a difference sitting on a line. Walking round a shape and coming home to
nothing. One setting that decides how much each line counts. One rule that pushes every number
towards its neighbours'. A tick too big for its shape, so the numbers run away instead of
sloshing. That is the list above in plain words, and none of it needs a name to be counted.

Borrowed names arrive in the prose after the thing they name; the grey box at the top of each
chapter lists them up front, so you know which words are on loan. A name is a handle, useful once
you are holding something. Set these aside.

## What you get

<!-- beat 2 -->

The ideas themselves by the last page, not a rumour of them. You will know what the people who
work on this know, and there is less of it than the vocabulary suggests.

You will be able to hold a whole world in your head and count it; run the rule everything else is
made of; say why the tick a world runs on belongs to its shape; read a machine's honest *no* and
tell it from a mistake; walk from any number we produced to where it came from — a file, or the
arithmetic worked out in front of you; and leave a note on the section that lost you — which needs
a free GitHub account, the only thing here that asks you to go and get anything.

You will not be asked to believe anything. Until the pencil runs out, every number you are asked
to check you can do by hand; after that, from the record that travels with the book.

One thing you will not have, and it matters most: a claim about the world. What we built is a toy
— small, exact, made on purpose — and a borrowed physics word names a feature of it.

## The road

<!-- beat 3 -->

After the shadow, five chapters happen on a napkin. Two dots and a line, then a triangle, then the
smallest solid there is — and that small thing turns out to be a whole world: every kind of number
it can hold, and one setting that decides how much each line counts. Then we make it move. You can
do all of that by hand, and you should.

In the last of the five the napkin visibly runs out: the smallest world with any room in it needs
arithmetic no hand can do. So the same rule, unchanged, goes to a machine, on a world with no edge
at all.

The rest is what came back. A ripple lopsided until one setting fixed it. What it costs to push on
a piece of it. A gap emptier than empty. A wall that worked and two hopes that did not. A
measurement with its answer key taken away, a law that came back refused, and a published miss
that was our own bookkeeping. The noes are kept on purpose and given the same room as the yeses,
because a machine that can only agree with you is not an instrument.

## How to read it

<!-- beat 4 -->

This is for someone curious who is not a physicist. There is nothing you have to *do* in it beyond
adding and taking away, which is not a simplification for your benefit but what the object needs.
Numbers a machine worked out will go past you; you are never asked to reproduce one by hand, only
to see what it was compared against — though the last chapter hands you the commands.

Read it in order. Each chapter earns the next one's question, and the book is short enough that
skipping ahead costs more than it saves.

And do the ✎ moments. From partway in, every chapter that runs an experiment stops just before the
answer and asks you to write your guess down. That pause is the method, performed instead of
described, and it only works if you commit: being wrong on paper is the point.

We start where measuring started: a shadow, and a man in Alexandria who did the smallest possible
next thing about one.
