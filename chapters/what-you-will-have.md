# What you will have

> **Scope.** This is a book about a **toy**: a small world built inside a computer, and what happened
> when we pointed real tests at it. Nothing here is a claim about nature. Where a chapter uses a word
> like *vacuum*, the word names a pattern in the model.

This is the one page that talks about the book instead of doing it. Two minutes, and then we begin.

## The thing this is about

<!-- beat 1 -->

There is a subject underneath this book, and it has a name: *discrete exterior calculus*. It is a
way of doing calculus on a shape made of dots, lines and faces instead of a smooth space. People
spend years learning it, under names that give a beginner nothing to hold on to. Forms. Exactness.
The Hodge star. The Laplacian. Stability.

Please do not go and look any of that up yet.

You will meet all five of those things in this book, and almost none of those words. A number
sitting on a dot, and a difference sitting on a line. Walking round a shape and coming home to
nothing. One setting that decides how much each line counts. One rule that pushes every number
towards its neighbours'. A tick too big for the shape it is ticking, so the numbers run away instead
of sloshing. That is the list above, in plain words, and none of it needs a name to be counted.

Two borrowed names do turn up later — *the vacuum* and *universality* — both after the thing they
name. A name is a handle, and handles are useful once you are holding something. Set
these five aside.

## What you get

<!-- beat 2 -->

The ideas themselves, by the last page — not a rumour of them. You will know what the people who
work on this know: there is less to know than the vocabulary suggests.

You will be able to hold a whole world in your head and count it; run the single rule everything
after it is made of; say why the tick a world runs on belongs to its shape; read a machine's honest
*no* and tell it from a mistake; walk from a number on a page to the file it came out of; and leave
a note on the section that lost you, which is the only thing in here that asks anything of you — a
free account where it is published.

You will not be asked to believe anything. Until the pencil runs out, every number you are asked to
check is one you can do by hand; after that they are quoted from a record that travels with the
book.

And one thing you will not have, which matters most: a claim about the world. What we built is a
toy — small, exact, made on purpose — and a word borrowed from physics names a feature of it.

## The road

<!-- beat 3 -->

The five chapters after this one happen on a napkin. Two dots and a line, then a triangle, then the
smallest solid there is — and that small thing turns out to be a whole world: every kind of number
it can hold, and one setting that decides how much each line counts. Then we make it move. You can
do all of that by hand, and you should.

In the last of the five the napkin visibly runs out: the smallest world with any room in it needs
arithmetic no hand can do. So the same rule, unchanged, goes to a machine, on a world with no edge
at all.

The rest is what came back. A ripple lopsided until one setting fixed it. What it costs to push on
a piece of it. A gap emptier than empty. A wall that worked and two hopes that did not. A
measurement with its answer key taken away, a law that came back refused, and a published miss that
was our own bookkeeping. The noes are kept on purpose and given the same room as the yeses, because
a machine that can only agree with you is not an instrument.

## How to read it

<!-- beat 4 -->

This is written for someone curious who is not a physicist. There is nothing you have to *do* in it
beyond adding and taking away — which is not a simplification for your benefit but what the object
actually needs. Numbers a machine worked out will go past you later on; you are never asked
to reproduce one, only to see what it was compared against.

Read it in order. Each chapter earns the next one's question, and the book is short enough that
skipping ahead costs more than it saves.

And do the ✎ moments. From partway in, every chapter that runs an experiment stops just before the
answer and asks you to write your guess down. That pause is the method, performed instead of
described, and it only works if you commit — being wrong on paper is the point of it, not the risk.

We start where measuring started: with a shadow, and a man in Alexandria who did the smallest
possible next thing about one.
