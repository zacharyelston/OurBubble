# When the expected law fails

> **Scope.** *Cavity*, *resonance*, *material weight* and *exponent* name geometry and numbers inside a
> lattice **toy**. No real substance is being modelled and no real frequency is being measured; the
> record's own name for this experiment, and what it was originally a bridge toward, are in the
> appendix. This chapter is about a prediction that came out wrong.

This book has reported plenty of noes already. The wall did not change inertia. A volume could not
be emptied. And once, a pair of noes turned out to be ours rather than the lattice's, and was
withdrawn.

This one is a different kind of no, and it is the kind that decides whether the others were worth
reading.

Every negative so far answered a *question* — can this be done? — and came back no, or came back no
and was later corrected. Here, for the first time, **the project proposed a law and its own
instrument refused it.** We said what the number would be, wrote it down, handed the data to a
machine that had never been told the answer, and the machine came back with something else.

That is the test a measuring device has to pass before any of its agreements mean anything.

## The setup, and the guess

Put a small region inside a resonating cavity and make it harder for the field to move through — a
lump of denser material, in effect. The resonance should drop in frequency. That much is not in
question.

The question is *by how much*, as the lump is made denser.

There is a simple argument that gives you an answer. If you filled the *whole* cavity with the
denser material, the frequency would fall like a square root: quadruple the material weight and the
frequency halves. So — the argument goes — putting some of that material in should do the same
thing, just less of it. The scaling should still be a square root.

It is a clean, plausible expectation, and it is specific enough to be wrong. We registered it before
running: the exponent should come out at **p = −1/2**.

## The part that worked

The experiment does what it says. Place the weighted region near a driven edge, sweep the drive
frequency, and find the peak. As the region gets denser, the peak moves down, smoothly and
repeatably. There is no ambiguity about whether there is an effect, and no ambiguity about its
direction.

So there is a law here worth measuring. We handed the peak positions and the weights to the same
blind fitter from [the round ripple](the-round-ripple.md) and
[the vacuum chapter](where-negative-energy-appears.md).

## The part that failed

It came back with **p = −0.2753**, at a fit quality of **R² = 0.9774**.

The expectation was −1/2. The measurement is a little over half that steep. This is not a near miss
inside an error bar; the law we proposed is the wrong law for this geometry, and it is refused.

## Why it failed, and why that is a question rather than a rescue

The square-root argument assumes the material weight governs the whole resonating volume. Here it
does not. The lump occupies a small part of the cavity and most of the vibration is still sitting in
unchanged space. A local change should not inherit the scaling of a global one — which, said
plainly, means the mistake was in the argument rather than in the run.

That explanation is comfortable, which is exactly when to be careful. So it is written down as a
*new prediction* rather than a patch: if the reason is really the volume fraction, then increasing
the lump's share of the cavity should move the exponent toward −1/2. If it does not, the explanation
is wrong too, and it will be a registered miss like the first one.

That is the difference between an explanation and an excuse. An explanation makes a new commitment.

## The same instrument, refusing

The same instrument that selected a straight-line law for the ripple, and recovered a coefficient
with a π in it from the vacuum, and produced an exponent nobody supplied for the critical point —
that same instrument, unchanged, refused the most natural material-scaling analogy anyone would have
reached for.

It returned the law that was in the data rather than the law in the chapter title.
