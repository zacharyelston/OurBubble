# 00 · The object — what is the least you need to hold a physics?

**Concept:** you can build a world out of two things — somewhere to put a number, and somewhere to
put a difference — and everything the rest of this book does happens on that one object. This
chapter builds it in eleven steps, each forced by the one before it, and does not name the object
until step ten.

> **Firewall.** Every word here describes a **toy** discrete-exterior-calculus computation on a
> lattice. *Photon, field, light cone, ring, speed, matter, antimatter* name properties of discrete
> operators and of fits to their output — never real photons, real light, real particles, or a
> measurement of any physical constant. Nothing in this chapter is a claim about nature.
> Dimensionless throughout; `c = 1` in code units only. See [`FIREWALL.md`](../../../FIREWALL.md).

**Source.** This chapter is the long-form of section 1 of the synthesis front door
([`viz/synthesis.html`](../../../viz/synthesis.html), generated by
[`core/viz/src/bin/gen_synthesis.rs`](../../../core/viz/src/bin/gen_synthesis.rs)). Its numbers come
from four places, each cited in place: the `d²=0` sweep and the tetrahedron angles are **computed at
page-generation time** by that program; the isotropy numbers are lab
[`warp-1-move/0115-lattice-matched-isotropy`](../../../lab/warp-1-move/0115-lattice-matched-isotropy)
(gate `uf1_5_lattice_matched_gate.rs`); the mesh-repair numbers are lab
[`warp-3-shield/0305-doubleslit-mirror`](../../../lab/warp-3-shield/0305-doubleslit-mirror) (gate
`uf3_5_doubleslit_mirror_gate.rs`); the two-sciences numbers are lab

## The hook  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Most explanations of computational physics begin in the middle. Here is a mesh, they say; here are
the equations; here is what we ran. If you already know why a mesh is the shape it is, that is
efficient. If you do not, it is a wall.

This chapter starts one step earlier than the middle, and then does not skip. **Eleven steps.** Each
one is forced by the one before it, in the sense that if you accept the previous step you cannot
reasonably refuse the next. Nothing is introduced because it will be needed later; everything is
introduced because the step before it left a hole.

The object at the end is called the **stella octangula**, and you will meet it in step ten. It
matters that it comes last. Almost every account of this project — including several earlier drafts
of the page this chapter is drawn from — opened by putting the object on the table and describing
it. That order teaches the object's *features*. This order teaches why it had to be *chosen*, which
is a different and more useful thing, because the choice turns out to have consequences you can
measure, and measuring them is most of what the rest of the book is about.

---

## Step 1 · What is the least you actually need?  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

The calculus that physics is written in assumes you can always look closer. Between any two points
there are infinitely many more; between any two of *those*, infinitely many more again. Every
derivative, every integral, every "let ε go to zero" rests on it.

**No measurement has ever seen that.** Every measurement ever made is a finite list of numbers.
Whether space itself is like the calculus is an old open question, and — this is the part worth
saying out loud — **this chapter does not need the answer.** Nothing below depends on it either way.

So do not start from a continuum and approximate it. Start from nothing, and ask what is the least
you need in order to hold a physics at all.

You need somewhere to **put a number**. Call that a **point**. And you need somewhere to put
*change*, because change is the thing physics is mostly about. A difference is never *at* one place —
it is *between* two. So a difference between two points lives on the **line joining them**.

A point and a line. That is the whole starting kit.

And here is the sentence the rest of the chapter rests on: a point and a line are **not a shrunken
picture of something richer**. They are a complete world of their own, and everything that follows on
them is *exact*. When a textbook continuum formula appears later in this book, it appears as a
**comparison** — never as the standard the lattice owes anything to. The lattice is not trying to be
a continuum badly. It is being itself, exactly.

> **The jargon** <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->: a number living on a point is a **0-form**; a number living
> on a line is a **1-form**. Nothing else has been assumed yet — not distance, not direction, not
> time. In the engine these are the vertex and edge arrays of a
> [`SimplicialComplex`](../../../core/geom/src/mesh.rs); the whole of Layer 0 (`geom`) is points,
> lines, and which bounds which.

---

## Step 2 · Go around a closed loop and the differences cancel — exactly  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Walk a loop of lines back to where you started, adding up the differences as you go. You must arrive
at zero, because you are back at the number you left.

Not *nearly* zero. **Zero.** The differences are added with plus and minus signs that come from
which way each line points, so the cancellation is a fact about **integers** — not the outcome of a
subtraction that happened to come out small.

This is the whole reason a machine built this way can be trusted *before* any physics is put into
it. Most simulation error is the slow rot of an identity that was only ever approximately true: it
holds at this resolution, in these units, for this many timesteps, and then it does not. Here the
identity is **combinatorial**. It holds at any resolution, in any units, on any mesh, at any
timestep — or the mesh is not a mesh.

### The check, run while the page was built  <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->

This is not asserted in the abstract. The program that builds the synthesis page constructs a small
instance of the object, sums every boundary walk in it at every point that walk touches, and refuses
to write the page if any total is non-zero:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| complex built | `geom::mesh_3d_chiral_tetoct(3, 3, 3, chiral_oct_screw111)` |
| (patch, point) checks summed | **372** |
| largest surviving total | **0** — as an integer, not below a tolerance |

*Source: `mesh_census()` in [`gen_synthesis.rs`](../../../core/viz/src/bin/gen_synthesis.rs),
re-executed on every page build. In the jargon this identity is `d² = 0`, and it is the reason
`d` and `⋆` are the only two structures the engine is allowed to have — see
[`GENESIS.md`](../../../GENESIS.md).*

## Step 3 · So far, nothing knows how long a line is  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Read step 2 again and notice what was never used: **length**. No line has one. Two lines cannot be
compared. There is no angle, no volume, no speed — and the loop identity does not care, because it
never asked.

That is not a gap to be filled in later. It is **why** the structure is exact. Counting and
orientation are all that step 2 consumed, so nothing about the shape of the world can make step 2
wrong. Geometry has to come from somewhere else, and keeping it somewhere else is the design.

---

## Step 4 · Nothing has moved yet. Make time.  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Everything so far is a still photograph. Numbers sit on points, differences sit on lines, and nothing
has happened — because there is no clock. So add one: **let there be ticks**, all the same size.

Then add exactly one rule for what a tick does:

> **At each tick, every point adjusts its own number toward its neighbours' — by an amount the
> differences on its lines tell it — and carries forward the change it has built up so far.**

That sentence is the whole law. **Nothing is ever added to it.** No forces, no particles, no separate
equation of motion bolted on later when something needs explaining. Everything in the rest of this
book — orbits, diffraction, electrostatics, the vacuum between plates, criticality, photonic bands,
tomography, random walks — is that one rule, run, on the object step 10 arrives at.

> **The jargon** <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->: the discrete scalar wave equation `φ̈ = −c² Δ₀ φ`, advanced
> by leapfrog. The engine's implementation is `step_scalar_wave` in
> [`core/solve/src/wave_solver.rs`](../../../core/solve/src/wave_solver.rs) — which is the file to
> read if you want to check that the paragraph above is the truth about the code and not a
> simplification of it. `Δ₀ = ⋆₀⁻¹D₀ᵀ⋆₁D₀`: two copies of `d` with the dial of step 7 between them,
> and nothing else.

---

## Step 5 · The rule makes ripples  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Start everything at rest and **poke a single point** — raise its number, then let go.

Its neighbours notice, because the differences on their lines just changed. Their neighbours notice
on the next tick. What comes out is a **ring spreading outward**, like a stone dropped in a pond.

Nobody wrote "wave" anywhere. There is no wave in the rule; the rule is one sentence about
neighbours. **The ring is what that sentence does.**

This is worth pausing on, because it is the first time the object does something nobody put into it,
and it is the pattern for everything later in the book. The engine is never told what phenomenon to
produce. It is told how neighbours settle, and the phenomenon is the consequence.

### Watch it  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

- **[▶ the double-slit demo](../../../viz/doubleslit.html)** — a poke, the rings, and two gaps. The
  engine steps it; the browser computes no physics and only draws what it is sent. With no server
  running it replays the same solver's committed export.
- **[▶ the pulse demo](../../../viz/em.html)** — the same rule as an expanding pulse, round and
  lopsided side by side. This is the picture step 7 is about.

And now you have the question a stopwatch asks, which turns out to be the first real question in this
whole project:

> **How fast is that ring travelling — and is it the same speed in every direction?**

---

## Step 6 · "How fast" needs "how far" — and no line has a length  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Speed is distance over time. The time half is easy: it is ticks, and every tick is the same. The
distance half you **do not have** — and step 3 was *proud* of that.

Counting hops does not rescue it. A step along a cube's edge and a step along its diagonal are both
**one hop**, and nothing anywhere in the structure says they should count differently. If they truly
don't, then "how fast" has no answer at all — only "how many hops", which is a different question,
and not one a stopwatch can check.

So sizes have to be written in somewhere. **Length is not something this object has; it is something
you have to give it** — and it has to enter through exactly *one* place, because steps 2 and 3 hold
precisely *because* nothing else knows about it.

The reader should now want the dial. That is the point of this step: the next one is not an assertion
you are asked to accept, it is the answer to a question you now have.

---

## Step 7 · The metric is a dial — and you can watch it being turned  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

**Here is that one place.** A single component, whose only job is to say how big things are: how much
each line counts for, how much each small piece of surface counts for. Set it and the object has a
geometry; set it differently and the object has a different one. It is a **dial**, and it is the
*only* dial.

Set it naively — every line weighted the same, the setting that looks like "no assumption" and is in
fact a strong one — and **step 5's ring comes out lopsided**: it runs faster along some directions
than others, so what ought to be a circle is a squashed one. Set the dial from the actual geometry
instead and the ring comes back round.

Draw that ring's speed in every direction and you have the thing physics calls a **light cone**. It
is a picture of the ring, and — firewall — nothing more than that.

This is not an argument. It is measured, twice.

| dial setting | speed along an axis | speed along a diagonal | anisotropy |
|---|---|---|---|
| naive (`⋆ = I`) | 0.6289 | 0.8109 | **22.4%** |
| from the geometry | 0.7843 | 0.8019 | **2.2%** |

**`anisotropy 22.4% → 2.2%, ×10.2`** — quoted verbatim from the ledger row. Both predictions were

### And now the consequence of step 1  <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->

There is **no truer geometry sitting behind this one** for the dial to be tuned toward. Nothing else
on this object assumes a length anywhere — that was step 3 — so the dial does not *approximate* a
geometry. It **supplies** the only one there is.

Two settings are therefore not a better and a worse version of the same thing. They are **two
different geometries**, and the difference shows up in measurements.

Which is why a whole arc of the record stops treating the dial as plumbing and makes it the object of
study. Rungs [`bridge 8.13` through `bridge 8.18`](../../../lab/bridge/README.md) engineer the dial
deliberately and measure what each choice costs — in isotropy, in positivity, in entropy. The last of
them puts a number on one of the trades: **`the entropy cost of exact isotropy is 3.771e-5`** (ledger
row `8.18`, lab [`bridge/0818-full-star-coopt`](../../../lab/bridge/0818-full-star-coopt), gate
`uf8_18_full_star_coopt_gate.rs`).

That is a sentence you can only write if you have accepted step 1. If the lattice were an
approximation of something truer, "the cost of exact isotropy" would be a measure of how badly you
were approximating. Here it is a property of a designed object.

## Step 8 · Finish the ladder before choosing any shape  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Step 1 stopped at a point and a line. Take one more rung, at the same walking pace.

A **patch** is a piece of surface with lines around its edge. Walk that edge, adding up the
line-numbers as you go — exactly the walk from step 2 — and you get one number. It is not a number
about any single line; it belongs to the **patch**. It is the amount of *circulation* the patch
encloses.

One more rung and you are done. A **volume** is a region with patches around its outside. Add up what
each of those patches carries and you get a number about the volume: how much is flowing *out* of it.

Now the physics words can land, and they land plainly. In electromagnetism the potential **A** is a
number on each line; the field strength **F** is a number on each patch. And they are not two things
you have to relate by a law — **the loop-sum of A around a patch simply IS the F on that patch.**
Same walk as step 2, one rung up.

> **The jargon** <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->: patches hold **2-forms**, volumes hold **3-forms**, and
> `F = dA`. The full chain is
>
> **point** `—d₀→` **line (the potential A)** `—d₁→` **patch (the field F = dA)** `—d₂→` **volume**
>
> Each arrow is "walk the boundary and add up", and step 2 is the statement that doing it twice in a
> row always gives nothing. *Firewall: `A` and `F` name edge and face arrays of a toy operator, never
> a real electromagnetic field.* The `A → F → E/B` chain is rendered directly from the engine's
> export at [`viz/afeb.html`](../../../viz/afeb.html).

## Step 9 · What shape should a patch be?  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Squares look like the obvious answer, and they are the wrong atom.

Pin the four corners of a square and the square can still **flex**: it leans over into a diamond
without any corner moving off its own pin, because nothing fixes its diagonals. A **triangle**
cannot. Fix its three corners and the triangle is finished — it is the smallest patch that holds its
own shape.

The same argument one rung up gives the **tetrahedron**: four corners, four triangular faces, rigid
for the same reason. So the honest atoms are triangles and tetrahedra.

> **The jargon** <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->: simplices — hence *simplicial* complex, hence the `geom`
> layer's [`SimplicialComplex`](../../../core/geom/src/mesh.rs). Rigidity here is the informal
> statement; the load-bearing version is that a simplex's geometry is determined by its edge lengths
> alone, which is exactly what lets the dial of step 7 be a function of edge and face measures and
> nothing else.

---

## Step 10 · And regular tetrahedra do not fill space  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

This is the surprise, and it is an old one. Aristotle wrote that they do, and the mistake stood for
roughly eighteen centuries before anyone measured the angle.

So measure it — on the very tetrahedron this book's figures draw. Stack tetrahedra around a shared
edge and they very nearly close, and *nearly* is the whole story:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| angle between two faces meeting along one edge | **70.5288°** |
| copies needed to close a full turn (360°) | **5.1043** — not a whole number |
| left over once 5 are packed in | **7.3561°** — a wedge nothing can fill |

*Computed by `tetra_packing()` in
[`gen_synthesis.rs`](../../../core/viz/src/bin/gen_synthesis.rs) from the constructed tetrahedron's
own coordinates, and asserted against the exact value `arccos(1/3)` before it is printed. Not
remembered — measured, on every page build.*

So there is no perfect answer, and **every tetrahedral lattice is a compromise.** That matters more
than it sounds. A compromise is a **choice**; a choice has consequences; and the consequences are
measurable — which, as step 7 already showed, is what most of this book turns out to be about. The
project says it in one line of [`GENESIS.md`](../../../GENESIS.md): *the stella-octangula is a choice
of lattice.*

### Here is the choice  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Take the cube's eight corners and two-colour them by whether their coordinates sum to an even or an
odd number. Each colour's four corners span a regular tetrahedron; the two tetrahedra interpenetrate
and **never share an edge**. That figure is the **stella octangula**.

Tile it, and the near-closure gap from the table above shows up as honest **octahedral holes**
between the tetrahedra — filled in their turn, each split into four more tetrahedra.

The builder's own description, from
[`core/geom/src/mesh.rs`](../../../core/geom/src/mesh.rs):

> a CHIRAL *tetrahedral–octahedral (alternated cubic / FCC)* honeycomb … **Odd-sum points are
> octahedral-hole centres, NOT vertices.**

The two families are mirror images of each other, so **chirality is not a feature added later** — it
is on the ground floor, before a single field is defined, which is why the chapters keep meeting it.

> **A naming warning, and it is the firewall's exact failure mode** <!-- [PUBLIC] -->: the engine's
> source calls the two families *matter* and *antimatter*. **That is a nickname for two sets of cube
> corners and nothing more.** It is not a model of matter, of antimatter, or of any asymmetry between
> them. The vocabulary is convenient and the temptation to read it as physics is exactly what
> [`FIREWALL.md`](../../../FIREWALL.md) exists to refuse.

## Step 11 · One object, two sciences — the whole book, in miniature  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

Before the rest of the book, here is the move it repeats, done once.

Put a blob on the lattice and let it spread using step 4's one rule — nothing else. Run it two ways.

**Let it leak.** The blob loses height as it widens, the way heat does. Its width grows in step with
the clock: double the time, double the spread.

**Let nothing leak.** Everything is kept, the way a quantum amplitude keeps its total. Now it does
not spread, it *travels*: double the time, quadruple the spread.

Two sciences, two different answers — and the machine that read the numbers was not told which one it
was looking at.

### The measurement  <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->

Lab [`bridge/0819-diffusion-discovery`](../../../lab/bridge/0819-diffusion-discovery), gate
`core/uniforge/tests/uf8_19_diffusion_discovery_gate.rs`, ledger row `bridge 8.19`:

| arm | law it should give | selected | measured exponent | margin over runner-up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| leaked (heat) | `⟨Δx²⟩ ∝ t` | spreading | **`p = 0.9999997`** | 0.2500 |
| conserved (quantum walk) | `⟨Δx²⟩ ∝ t²` | travelling | **`p = 2.0000000`** | 0.0625 |

**`same generator, same lattice, same start`** — the ledger's own words. The only difference between
the two arms is one factor of *i*, and the machine comes back with a **different correct law** rather
than repeating the one it just found. That is what makes the first answer worth anything: a
measurement device that returns the same answer whatever you show it has measured nothing.

Both arms also share their constant — the lattice's own neighbour sum, `6` in code units — appearing
once as the spreading rate and once as the travelling acceleration. And the rung registered its own
limit in advance: at long times the finite torus bends the leaked law down to `p = 0.9794`, which it
did.

### The hand-off  <!-- [PUBLIC] -->

**That is the whole book.** Every chapter after this one is the same move again: one object, one set
of operators, a different question asked of it — and, each time, a control that was free to give the
answer away.

No new engine is built for any of them. That is not an efficiency claim; it is the reason the
controls mean anything. A project with a different object per science could never be caught out by
one, because there would always be a reason this science's object behaved differently. Here there is
one object, and when a control comes back wrong, something is actually wrong.

---

## What happened

**In plain terms** <!-- [PUBLIC] -->: a point, a line, and a loop identity that is exact as integers
get you a world with no geometry in it. A clock and one rule get you ripples. Asking how fast a
ripple travels forces you to write sizes in, through exactly one dial — and turning that dial is
measurable, which is most of the science in this book. Triangles rather than squares, because
triangles hold their shape. Tetrahedra do not tile space, so the lattice is a compromise, and the
compromise chosen here is the stella octangula: two mirror tetrahedra with octahedral holes between
them, chirality present before any field is defined.

**The numbers** <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->: everything quoted in this chapter, and where it comes from.

| step | quantity | value | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | `d²=0` checks / worst total | 372 / **0** (integer) | `mesh_census()`, every page build |
| 7 | anisotropy, naive → geometric | **22.4% → 2.2%, ×10.2** | ledger `1.5` · lab `warp-1-move/0115` · `uf1_5` |
| 7 | demo asymmetry / repair | **~3.8×** / **5e-15** | ledger `3.5` · lab `warp-3-shield/0305` · `uf3_5` |
| 7 | entropy cost of exact isotropy | **3.771e-5** | ledger `8.18` · lab `bridge/0818` · `uf8_18` |
| 10 | dihedral / copies / leftover | **70.5288°** / **5.1043** / **7.3561°** | `tetra_packing()`, asserted vs `arccos(1/3)` |
| 10 | census (v / e / f / tets) | 32 / 112 / 124 / 43 | `mesh_census()` |

## Reproduce

```bash
cd core

# step 2's d²=0 sweep, step 10's angles and census, and every quoted ledger row —
# the generator asserts each quotation is present in its source file before writing the page
cargo run --release -p viz --bin gen_synthesis -- "$(git rev-parse --short HEAD)"

# step 7, measurement one — the isotropy numbers and figure
cargo test --release -p uniforge --test uf1_5_lattice_matched_gate -- --nocapture

# step 7, measurement two — the mesh repair
cargo test --release -p uniforge --test uf3_5_doubleslit_mirror_gate -- --nocapture
cargo run  --release -p viz     --bin gen_doubleslit_mirror

# step 11 — the two sciences
cargo test --release -p uniforge --test uf8_19_diffusion_discovery_gate -- --nocapture
cargo run  --release -p viz     --bin gen_diffusion
```

Then, from the **repo root**, `python3 -m http.server 8080` and open
`localhost:8080/viz/synthesis.html`. Pinned in [`PROVENANCE.md`](PROVENANCE.md).

overwrite its own committed data, then ask `git status --porcelain lab/` whether anything changed. It
should print nothing. See [`docs/DEMOS.md`](../../../docs/DEMOS.md) § *Demo 0 — the skeptic's test*.

## Try it  <!-- [STUDY GUIDE] -->

- **Break step 2.** Flip one incidence sign in a mesh builder and re-run `gen_synthesis`. The page
  refuses to build. Notice that this is a *combinatorial* failure — no tolerance was loosened, no
  timestep shortened; the integer identity simply stopped holding.
- **Turn the dial yourself.** Open [`viz/doubleslit.html`](../../../viz/doubleslit.html) with no
  server running and tick the crossed-diagonal box. Predict the direction of the change before you
  tick it: which side of the screen gets brighter, and why?
- **Predict step 10's leftover.** Before reading the table, work out how many regular tetrahedra fit
  around an edge from `arccos(1/3)` alone. Then ask the harder question: what would this book look
  like if the answer had been a whole number?
- **Find the third science.** Steps 4–11 used the rule twice (leaked, conserved). Pick any cell of the
  [science map](../../../viz/synthesis.html#s-map) and work out which three choices were made — form
  degree, wrapped shape, number of dimensions — to turn the same object into that experiment.
