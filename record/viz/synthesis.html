One lattice, many sciences

One object. Twelve different sciences were pointed at it, and each panel below is one of them. The results that came back no are printed at the same size as the ones that came back yes. If you have not met any of this before, start at section 1 and read down: it begins before the maths, with a point and a line.

The claim is not that one piece of maths turns up in many sciences. That is a textbook fact and nobody needs a page for it. The claim is that one exact, certified, bit-reproducible instrument spans them: each science below was entered with its prediction written down first, run against controls that were free to give the answer away, and reported at full size when it came back negative. The discipline is the product; the physics is what it was pointed at.

FIREWALL. Everything here is a toy DEC computation on a lattice. Photon, charge, light cone, Coulomb, vacuum, diffraction, material, orbit name numerical properties of discrete operators and of fits to their output — never real photons, charges, particles, forces, or measurements of any physical constant. What is prediction-grade is a set of dimensionless exponents and ratios, each pre-registered before its own run (R1). c = G = 1 in code units only.
This page is a demo artifact, not a new result. It registers no prediction, runs nothing and claims nothing of its own. It is generated from the committed record — PREDICTIONS.md's ledger rows, each chapter's rung log, each entry's eval.md verdict line, and the run CSVs. 1 · Start here — eleven steps to the object

Before any physics, and before any symbol. Eleven steps, each one forced by the step before it, ending at the thing every other section on this page runs on.

1

What is the least you actually need?

The calculus physics is written in assumes you can always look closer — infinitely many points between any two. No measurement has ever seen that: every measurement ever made is a finite list of numbers. Whether space itself is like the calculus is an old open question, and this page does not need the answer.

So don't start from a continuum and approximate it. Start from nothing, and ask what is the least you need to hold a physics. Somewhere to put a number — call that a point. Somewhere to put change — a difference is never at one place, it is between two, so it lives on the line joining them.

A point and a line. Not a shrunken picture of something richer — a complete world of its own, on which everything that follows is exact. When a textbook continuum formula appears on this page, it appears as a comparison, never as the standard the lattice owes anything to.

Nothing else has been assumed yet — not distance, not direction, not time. In the jargon: a number living on a point is a 0-form; a number living on a line is a 1-form.

2

Go around a closed loop and the differences cancel — exactly

Walk a loop of lines back to where you started, adding up the differences as you go. You must arrive at zero, because you are back at the number you left. Not nearly zero: zero. The differences are added with plus and minus signs that come from which way each line points, so the cancellation is a fact about integers, not the outcome of a subtraction that happened to be small.

This is the whole reason a machine built this way can be trusted before any physics is put into it. Most simulation error is the slow rot of an identity that was only ever approximately true. Here the identity is combinatorial: it holds at any resolution, in any units, on any mesh, at any timestep — or the mesh is not a mesh. In the jargon: d² = 0.

3

So far, nothing knows how long a line is

Read step 2 again and notice what was never used: length. No line has one. Two lines cannot be compared. There is no angle, no volume, no speed — and the loop identity does not care, because it never asked.

That is not a gap to be filled in later. It is why the structure is exact. Counting and orientation are all that step 2 consumed, so nothing about the shape of the world can make step 2 wrong. Geometry has to come from somewhere else, and keeping it somewhere else is the design.

4

Nothing has moved yet. Make time.

Everything so far is a still photograph. Numbers sit on points, differences sit on lines, and nothing has happened — because there is no clock. So add one: let there be ticks, all the same size.

Then add exactly one rule for what a tick does. At each tick, every point adjusts its own number toward its neighbours' — by an amount the differences on its lines tell it — and carries forward the change it has built up so far.

That sentence is the whole law. Nothing is ever added to it: no forces, no particles, no separate equation of motion bolted on later when something needs explaining. Everything further down this page is that one rule, run. In the jargon: the discrete wave equation , stepped by the engine's .

5

The rule makes ripples

Start everything at rest and poke a single point — raise its number, then let go. Its neighbours notice, because the differences on their lines just changed. Their neighbours notice on the next tick. What comes out is a ring spreading outward, like a stone dropped in a pond.

Nobody wrote "wave" anywhere. There is no wave in the rule — the rule is one sentence about neighbours. The ring is what that sentence does. You can watch it happen:

And now you have the question a stopwatch asks, which turns out to be the first real question in this whole project: how fast is that ring travelling — and is it the same speed in every direction?

6

"How fast" needs "how far" — and no line has a length

Speed is distance over time. The time half is easy: it is ticks, and every tick is the same. The distance half you do not have — and step 3 was proud of that.

Counting hops is not distance. A step along a cube's edge and a step along its diagonal are both one hop, and nothing anywhere in the structure says they should count differently. If they truly don't, then "how fast" has no answer at all — only "how many hops", which is a different question, and not one a stopwatch can check.

So sizes have to be written in somewhere. Length is not something this object has; it is something you have to give it — and it has to enter through exactly one place, because steps 2 and 3 hold precisely because nothing else knows about it.

7

The metric is a dial — and you can watch it being turned

Here is that one place. A single component, whose only job is to say how big things are: how much each line counts for, how much each small piece of surface counts for. Set it and the object has a geometry; set it differently and the object has a different one. It is a dial, and it is the only dial. In the jargon: the Hodge star, ⋆.

Set it naively — every line weighted the same, ⋆ = I, the thing that looks like "no assumption" and is in fact a strong one — and step 5's ring comes out lopsided: it runs faster along some directions than others, so what ought to be a circle is a squashed one. Set the dial from the actual geometry instead and the ring comes back round. (Draw that ring's speed in every direction and you have the thing physics calls a light cone — it is a picture of the ring, and nothing more than that.) This is not an argument; it is measured, twice, and both measurements are on this page:

The second one is worth clicking — and notice that it is the same ring all the way down: the ring you poked in step 5 is the ring this dial rounds out, and it is also the physics behind the double-slit demo's before/after toggle. That demo was quietly lopsided for months; the same dial explains it, and it now carries the toggle so you can turn the dial yourself. A lattice can be wrong, and the honest thing is to show you where.

Now read that against step 1. There is no truer geometry sitting behind this one for the dial to be tuned toward: nothing else on this object assumes a length anywhere, so the dial does not approximate a geometry — it supplies the only one there is. Two settings are therefore not a better and a worse version of the same thing; they are two different geometries, and the difference shows up in measurements. 8

Finish the ladder before choosing any shape

That was a long way into the record for one step, so come back to the construction. Two rungs are still missing, and both are needed before the shape of the object can be settled.

Step 1 stopped at a point and a line. Take one more rung at the same walking pace. A patch is a piece of surface with lines around its edge. Walk that edge, adding up the line-numbers as you go — exactly the walk from step 2 — and you get one number. It is not a number about any single line; it belongs to the patch. It is the amount of circulation the patch encloses.

One more rung and you are done. A volume is a region with patches around its outside. Add up what each of those patches carries and you get a number about the volume: how much is flowing out of it.

Now the physics words can land, and they land plainly. In electromagnetism the potential A is a number on each line; the field strength F is a number on each patch. And they are not two things you have to relate by a law — the loop-sum of A around a patch simply IS the F on that patch. Same walk as step 2, one rung up. In the jargon: patches hold 2-forms, volumes hold 3-forms, and F = dA.

9

What shape should a patch be?

Squares look like the obvious answer, and they are the wrong atom. Pin the four corners of a square and the square can still flex: it leans into a diamond without any corner moving off its own pin, because nothing fixes its diagonals. A triangle cannot. Fix its three corners and the triangle is finished — it is the smallest patch that holds its own shape.

The same argument one rung up gives the tetrahedron: four corners, four triangular faces, rigid for the same reason. So the honest atoms are triangles and tetrahedra. In the jargon: simplices — hence simplicial.

11

One object, two sciences — the whole page, in miniature

Before the twelve-cell map, here is the move it repeats, done once. Put a blob on the lattice and let it spread using step 4's one rule — nothing else. Run it two ways.

Let it leak — the blob loses height as it widens, the way heat does — and its width grows in step with the clock: double the time, double the spread. Let nothing leak — everything is kept, the way a quantum amplitude keeps its total — and it does not spread, it travels: double the time, quadruple the spread. Two sciences, two different answers, and the machine was not told which one it was looking at:

Same operator, same mesh, same starting blob. The only difference is one factor of i, and the bench comes back with a different correct law rather than repeating the one it just found — which is what makes the first answer worth anything.

That is the whole page. Every cell below is this same move again: one object, one set of operators, a different question asked of it — and, each time, a control that was free to give the answer away. No new engine is built for any of them, which is exactly why a control can catch this one out.

3 · How the answers were chosen

Every experiment above ends with a number, and a number is easy to get right by accident. So the numbers are not read off by hand. They go to one machine whose job is to look at a table and say what law it is looking at — and that machine is never told what it is looking at.

Here is exactly what "never told" means. Before a run, someone writes down the menu the machine is allowed to choose from — flat, spreading, straight-line, three halves, squared, cubed — and then hands over nothing but the raw pairs of numbers. There is no slot in which an expected answer could be passed in. The machine picks whichever item on the menu the data itself prefers, and the margin column says how far ahead of the runner-up that winner finished — so a near-tie shows up as a near-tie instead of being rounded into a victory.

The other half is the arms that are supposed to come back different. The same machine is fed cases whose right answer is not the one it just found: a deliberately broken metric, two charges instead of one, a walk that keeps everything instead of leaking, and a made-up law with no lattice in it at all. Each time, it has to return the other right answer. A machine that only ever agrees with you is not a test of anything.

One row below is one arm of one run — one direction, one window, one experiment — read straight out of that run's committed file and rounded for reading, so these are renderings of the file rather than quotations from it. That is why two numbers for the light cone are not a contradiction: the 0.9837 here is direction [100] on its own, and the p = 1.00 ± 0.016 in the map above is the summary across all five directions. Same run, different scope.

Fitted values are shown to four significant figures. R² — how well the chosen law fits, where 1 would be perfect — is rounded down, never up, so a fit that is not perfect can never print as 1.000. Where you see 1.000, the run's own file records the fit as exactly 1. Full precision lives in the files named at the foot of this page.

5 · What a passing test permits

A gate is an automated test that re-runs an experiment and refuses to pass unless the answer comes back as registered. This table asks a narrow question about each one: supposing that gate is passing, what does it actually entitle anyone to go and try next?

Read every row in the conditional. IF the gate is green, THEN some piece of work may be attempted — that is the whole claim. Not that the work will succeed, not that anything has been built, and never that something is true of the real world. The wording is deliberately cautious because the failure mode it guards against is the ordinary one: quietly treating a passing test as permission to claim more than it measured. Two rows license nothing at all, and they are kept rather than deleted — one because its experiment came back negative, one because the owner cancelled it. A table you can only add successes to is a menu, not a record. (The record calls this the subjunctive lane, because every row stays in the conditional mood and none of them is allowed to slip into the indicative. Tracked as issue #70.)

6 · The skeptic's test

Everything above is a page telling you about a record. Here is how to stop taking its word for it, in about the time it takes to make coffee.

Step 2 is the one that matters. Step 1 re-runs a real experiment and lets it overwrite the very files this page has been quoting. Step 2 then asks the version control system a single question: did anything change? If any number here had been nudged, tuned, or typed in after the fact, that is where it shows up. Computed here, while the page was being written: the loop identity's 372 checks, the object's census, and step 10's tetrahedron angles — those are not quoted from anywhere, they are worked out and checked against the exact value.

Read from a committed file but not cross-checked: the fitted exponents, R² and margins in section 3's table. And everything else here is prose. Every framing question, every grey line explaining what a control means, every word of the table in section 5 — written by a person, checked by nobody.
